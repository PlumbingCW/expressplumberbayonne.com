Commercial plumbing emergency: what we handle after hours.

Commercial plumbing covers the pipes, drains, heaters and fixtures in shops, restaurants, offices and multi-unit buildings. The systems are larger than a house, the lines run farther and a failure often affects more than one tenant.

Typical emergency calls are a backed-up floor drain or grease line, a burst supply pipe, a failed water heater, a toilet or urinal overflow, and a leak in a ceiling or wall that reaches the unit below. Close the nearest isolation valve and call. Delay raises the damage and the downtime.

Kitchen and grease lines.

Fat and food waste build up in drains and traps, then back up into the kitchen.

Restroom failures.

Overflowing toilets, running urinals and clogged floor drains can close a restroom.

Water heater failure.

A commercial heater that fails stops dishwashing, cleaning and hand washing.

Ceiling and wall leaks.

A leak in a shared riser can affect neighboring units.

Commercial work follows the same state plumbing subcode, a published standard, and the master plumber rule, with permits and inspections for new installs. The subcode also sets cross-connection and backflow rules that point to New Jersey Department of Environmental Protection requirements.

Backflow testing, grease traps and planned maintenance.

Backflow prevention devices protect the public water supply from contamination when pressure changes. Many commercial properties have them on the water service, irrigation or fire lines, and they must be tested on schedule. Ask your local water utility and the building department which tests apply to your building.

Grease traps and interceptors need regular cleaning so fat does not reach the sewer. A scheduled service costs less than an emergency backup on a busy night. For stubborn lines, hydro jetting clears a full pipe.

Leak detection in commercial buildings: what to expect.

Commercial leak detection is harder than in a house, because pipes run longer, are shared and are hidden above ceilings and behind finished walls. Detection tools help, but the plumber may need records of the building's plumbing and access to several units. It is not always possible to find a leak in one visit.

If you are a tenant, tell the building owner or manager as soon as you see a leak. Responsibility for pipes in walls, risers and shared lines often sits with the owner, while the tenant is responsible for fixtures in the unit. Check your lease. See leak detection for the methods used.

Business insurance varies widely. Property and business interruption cover sudden water damage in many policies, but not always sewer backup or gradual leaks. Check with your agent and photograph the damage and the downtime.
