How to Plumb a Double Sink Vanity: A Bayonne, NJ Guide

A double sink vanity gives two people their own basin, but it also doubles the plumbing. There are two faucets, two sets of supply lines and two drains that must join into a single pipe without trapping water or pulling the traps dry.

This guide explains the layout, the steps in order and the points where a first install often fails. It also says when a plumber should take over, because a vanity that is wrong in the wall is expensive to fix later.

How a double vanity drain is laid out.

Each basin has its own tailpiece and its own trap. The two traps then feed one shared drain pipe that goes into the wall. A trap is the curved section that holds a small plug of water, and that water stops sewer gas from rising into the room.

There are two common ways to join the drains. One uses a continuous waste kit, which is a horizontal pipe that links both tailpieces and drops into a single trap. The other gives each basin its own trap and joins them with a sanitary tee before the wall. Either way, the shared line must slope gently toward the wall so water keeps moving.

Step by step: from empty cabinet to working sinks.

  1. Close the shutoff valves for the old vanity, or the main valve if there are none, then open the faucet to drain the lines.
  2. Disconnect the old supply lines and trap, remove the old vanity and clean the wall and floor around the stub-outs.
  3. Set the new cabinet in place and level it. Mark and cut the openings for the drain and supply lines if the back of the cabinet needs them.
  4. Set the countertop and basins, then mount each faucet and pop-up assembly following the maker's instructions.
  5. Connect the supply lines. Use new braided lines and tighten them by hand plus a small turn with a wrench.
  6. Assemble the tailpieces, the drain kit and the traps. Keep every horizontal run sloping toward the wall and avoid sharp bends.
  7. Open the shutoff valves slowly, run both faucets and check every joint with a dry paper towel. Fill both basins and let them drain together.

A common mistake is to tighten a plastic slip nut with a wrench until it cracks. Hand tight plus a small turn is usually enough. Another is to forget the washers, which leaves a slow drip you will not notice until the cabinet floor swells.

Problems that show up after the install.

Symptom.Likely cause.Fix.
Gurgling when both basins drain.Poor venting or a trap too far from the vent.Have a plumber check the vent.
One basin drains slowly.Wrong slope or a kinked trap arm.Reset the slope.
Drip under the cabinet.Loose slip nut or missing washer.Tighten or replace the washer.
Sewer smell.Trap dried out or poorly assembled.Run water and check the trap assembly.
Low water flow at one faucet.Kinked supply line or debris in the aerator.Straighten the line and clean the aerator.

Gurgling is a sign that air is not entering the system behind the water. It is a vent problem, and it lives inside the wall. If the drain stays slow after you reset the traps, a drain cleaning clears what is in the line.

When a plumber is the better choice.

  • The old vanity had a single drain and the new one is a double.
  • The wall stub is corroded, loose or too far from the new drain position.
  • The supply valves do not close fully or are stuck.
  • You are moving the vanity or changing the wall plumbing.
  • The cabinet floor is soft, which suggests an earlier slow leak.

A plumber can move a stub-out, replace tired valves and test the vent. See our plumbing installation page for what we handle, and faucet repair if the new faucets drip or run weak.

Choosing parts before you start.

Buy the drain kit that matches your cabinet. Some vanities have a back panel and a drawer that block a standard trap. A kit designed for double vanities saves cutting and leaves room for the drawers.

Choose pop-up drain assemblies that match the faucets, and braided supply lines long enough to reach without a tight bend. A kinked line restricts flow and wears out sooner.

Test before you close the cabinet.

Run both faucets together for several minutes with paper towels under every joint. Fill both basins and release them at once to see how the shared drain copes. Check again after a day.

Only then restore the stored items. A slow drip found on day one costs a washer, and one found a month later costs a cabinet.
